When you describe what someone looks like in Swedish, you build sentences with
either **är** ("to be") or **har** ("to have"). Height, build, age and looks all use
**är**: **lång** (tall), **kort** (short), **medellång** (medium height), **smal**
(slim), **kraftig** (heavyset), **ung** (young), **gammal** (old), **snygg**
(good-looking).

Hair and eyes, on the other hand, use **har** — in Swedish you "have" hair and eyes
rather than "being" them. **Hår** is an ett-word, so any color adjective describing it
takes its ett-form: **blont hår** (blonde hair), **brunt hår** (brown hair). The same
agreement applies to texture: **lockigt hår** (curly hair) and **rakt hår**
(straight hair).

```example
Hon har långt, lockigt hår. — She has long, curly hair.
Han har kort, rakt hår. — He has short, straight hair.
```

Eyes work a little differently, because **ögon** ("eyes") is plural, so the color
adjective takes its plural form instead: **blå ögon** (blue eyes), **bruna ögon**
(brown eyes). The word for eye color is **ögonfärg**, and the word for hair color is
**hårfärg** — both are common questions: "Vilken hårfärg/ögonfärg har du?"

Facial hair has its own words: **skägg** (beard) and **mustasch** (moustache) are
ordinary nouns you "have" — **han har skägg**. **Glasögon** (glasses) is special: it
exists only in the plural, with no singular form at all. You never say "ett glasögon" —
instead, "a pair of glasses" is **ett par glasögon**.

Finally, there's the expression on someone's face: the verb **le** ("to smile") is
irregular (le, ler, log, lett), and its noun form is **leende** (a smile).

```example
Erik har grått hår och ett grått skägg. — Erik has grey hair and a grey beard.
Anna är ung och smal, och hon ler ofta. — Anna is young and slim, and she often smiles.
```

Put these pieces together — height, build, age, hair, eyes, facial hair, and
expression — and you can answer the question **"Hur ser han/hon ut?"** ("What does
he/she look like?") with a full, natural description.
