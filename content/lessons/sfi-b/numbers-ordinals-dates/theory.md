You already know 0-20. Beyond that, Swedish numbers build from a small set of
building blocks: **tjugo** (20), **trettio** (30), **fyrtio** (40), **femtio**
(50), up through **hundra** (100) and **tusen** (1000). Compound numbers are
written and said as one word, with no "and".

```example
tjugoen — twenty-one
trettio — thirty
hundra — a hundred
tvåhundra — two hundred
tusen — a thousand
```

Larger numbers just chain these blocks together: **hundrafemtio** (150) is
literally "hundred-fifty", and **tvåhundrasjuttio** (270) is "two-hundred-
seventy".

**Ordinal numbers** (first, second, third...) are a separate short list to
learn. Most end in **-e**, but the first ones are irregular:

| cardinal | ordinal |
|---|---|
| en/ett | första |
| två | andra |
| tre | tredje |
| fyra | fjärde |
| fem | femte |
| ... | ... |
| tio | tionde |

Ordinals are used for floors, positions in a list — and for **dates**. A date
always uses **den** + the ordinal + the month name:

```example
Idag är det den tredje maj. — Today is the third of May.
Vilket datum är det idag? — What is the date today?
Mötet är den tionde juni. — The meeting is on the tenth of June.
```

**Prices** in kronor use the plain cardinal number followed by **kronor**:

```example
Vad kostar den? — How much does it cost?
Det kostar hundra kronor. — It costs a hundred kronor.
Jackan kostar tvåhundra kronor. — The jacket costs two hundred kronor.
```

One useful shortcut: to ask a price, just say **Vad kostar...?** ("What does...
cost?") — it works for anything, from a coffee to a bike.
