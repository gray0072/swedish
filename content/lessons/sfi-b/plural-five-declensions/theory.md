In sfi-a you learned to just *recognise* a plural. Now let's systematise it: Swedish
nouns fall into **five plural patterns** (declensions). The ending doesn't depend
only on gender — it must be learned together with each noun.

| declension | ending | example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **-or** | en flicka -> flickor |
| 2 | **-ar** | en bil -> bilar |
| 3 | **-er** | en lägenhet -> lägenheter |
| 4 | **-n** | ett äpple -> äpplen |
| 5 | (none) | ett hus -> hus |

```example
en flicka — flickor — a girl — girls
en bil — bilar — a car — cars
en lägenhet — lägenheter — an apartment — apartments
ett äpple — äpplen — an apple — apples
ett hus — hus — a house — houses
```

A few more examples per pattern:

```example
en gata — gator — a street — streets
en hund — hundar — a dog — dogs
en biljett — biljetter — a ticket — tickets
ett hjärta — hjärtan — a heart — hearts
ett bord — bord — a table — tables
```

Some tendencies help as rough guides: many **en-words ending in -a** take **-or**
(flicka -> flickor); many other **en-words** take **-ar** (bil -> bilar); words
borrowed or ending in certain consonant clusters often take **-er** (lägenhet ->
lägenheter); most **ett-words ending in a vowel** take **-n** (äpple -> äpplen); and
most **ett-words ending in a consonant** take **no ending at all** (hus -> hus,
barn -> barn).

But these are tendencies, not laws — there is no rule that lets you derive the
plural with certainty just from the singular. The declension has to be learned as
part of the word itself, the same way you learned en/ett. Whenever you meet a new
noun, learn all three pieces together: its gender, its definite singular, and its
indefinite plural.
