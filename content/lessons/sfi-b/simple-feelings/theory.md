Two simple ways to describe how you feel: **vara** ("to be") plus an adjective,
or **känna sig** ("to feel", literally "feel oneself") plus an adjective. Often
either one works: **Jag är trött** and **Jag känner mig trött** both mean "I am
tired," though **känna sig** puts a little more weight on the feeling itself,
right now, rather than a lasting state.

```example
Jag är trött. — I am tired.
Jag känner mig trött. — I feel tired.
```

The adjective agrees with what it describes, exactly like **röd** becomes
**rött** before an ett-word. Most feeling-words follow the regular pattern: add
**-t** for ett-words, **-a** for the plural. **Glad** → glatt, glada.
**Stressad** → stressat, stressade. **Orolig** → oroligt, oroliga.

A handful of adjectives ending in **-en** are irregular: **ledsen** ("sad")
becomes **ledset**, **ledsna** — not "ledsent" or "ledsena". **Besviken**
("disappointed") follows the same shape: besviket, besvikna.

```example
Barnet är ledset. — The child is sad.
Vi är besvikna på resultatet. — We are disappointed with the result.
```

For basic needs, plain **vara** is the natural choice: **Jag är hungrig** (I am
hungry), **Jag är törstig** (I am thirsty). You would rarely hear "känner mig
hungrig" for ordinary hunger — not wrong, just less common than the simple
**är**.

**Känna sig** shines with more situational or momentary feelings: nervousness,
calm, worry. Notice the reflexive pronoun **mig** — it must agree with the
subject: jag känner **mig**, du känner **dig**, hon känner **sig**.

```example
Jag känner mig nervös inför provet. — I feel nervous before the test.
Han känner sig lugn nu. — He feels calm now.
```

One quirk worth a mention: the ett-form of **rädd** ("scared") is technically
**rätt** — identical to the unrelated word for "correct" — but Swedes almost
never actually say it; they rephrase instead ("ett skrämt barn" rather than
"ett rädd/rätt barn").
