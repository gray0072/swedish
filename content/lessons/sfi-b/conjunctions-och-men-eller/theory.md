Swedish has five common coordinating conjunctions. They join two words, or two
independent (main) clauses of equal weight: **och** (and), **men** (but),
**eller** (or), **för** (because — a casual, added reason), and **så** (so,
a result).

```example
Jag dricker kaffe och äter en smörgås. — I drink coffee and eat a sandwich.
Jag vill sova, men jag måste jobba. — I want to sleep, but I must work.
Vill du ha te eller kaffe? — Do you want tea or coffee?
Jag är trött, för jag sov dåligt. — I'm tired, because I slept badly.
Det regnar, så jag tar paraply. — It's raining, so I take an umbrella.
```

Notice **för** here — it adds a casual extra reason, close to English "for" or
"since" in old-fashioned speech. Formal Swedish also has **eftersom**
("because"), a subordinating conjunction with different word order — that one
comes in a later lesson.

The key rule for all five words above: the clause that follows keeps ordinary
**main-clause word order** — subject before the verb, just like a sentence
standing on its own (this is called V2 order). Nothing gets inverted:

```example
..., men jag har inte tid. — ..., but I don't have time.
..., så jag tar en paraply. — ..., so I take an umbrella.
```

In both examples **jag** comes before the verb, exactly as it would if you
started a brand-new sentence. This is different from subordinating
conjunctions like *eftersom*, *att* or *om*, which change where the verb goes
— not covered in this lesson.

You can also use these words to join just two smaller pieces, not whole
clauses:

```example
bröd och smör — bread and butter
stor eller liten — big or small
```

So the rule of thumb is simple: pick the right conjunction for the meaning you
want (addition, contrast, choice, reason, result), place it between the two
parts, and leave the word order on both sides completely untouched.
