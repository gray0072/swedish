When you sit down at a restaurant in Sweden, a **servitör** (waiter) or **servitris**
(waitress) usually brings the **meny** (menu) and asks if you'd like to **beställa**
(order) something to drink first. A full meal is often split into three parts:
**förrätt** (starter), **huvudrätt** (main course) and **efterrätt** (dessert) — though
for a quick lunch many people just order the huvudrätt.

```example
Kan jag få se menyn, tack? — Can I see the menu, please?
Jag vill beställa en kopp kaffe. — I would like to order a cup of coffee.
Vad rekommenderar du? — What do you recommend?
```

Before you start eating, it's polite to say **smaklig måltid** ("enjoy your meal") —
the Swedish version of *bon appétit*. When everyone in the group wants to pay for
their own food, you can say you'll **betala var för sig** (pay separately, split the
bill); otherwise one person simply asks for **notan** (the bill) for the whole table.

```example
Smaklig måltid! — Enjoy your meal!
Kan vi få notan, tack? — Can we have the bill, please?
Vi betalar var för sig. — We're paying separately.
```

A **kvitto** (receipt) is not the same as the bill — you ask for it only after you've
already paid, usually to keep for your own records. Leaving **dricks** (a tip) is
appreciated but not expected the way it is in some other countries; rounding up the
amount is common enough.

No lesson about Swedish eating habits is complete without **fika** — both a noun and
a verb for the beloved coffee break, ideally with a **kanelbulle** (cinnamon bun) on
the side. Fika is less about the coffee itself and more about pausing to sit down
with colleagues or friends; Swedes fika at work, at home, and on every café visit. If
you want to reserve a table ahead of a busy evening, remember to **boka bord** (book
a table) — a **gäst** (guest) who just walks in on a Friday night may have to wait.
