You already know that a single Swedish word has one loud syllable. Now zoom out
to the whole sentence: a statement and a yes/no question have different
melodies, even with the exact same words.

A **statement**'s pitch rises a little in the middle and then falls at the
very end.

```example
Du bor i Malmö. — You live in Malmö. (pitch falls at the end)
Hon pratar svenska. — She speaks Swedish. (pitch falls at the end)
```

A **yes/no question** does the opposite: the pitch climbs and stays up, or
rises further, right at the end. Word order also flips — the verb jumps in
front of the subject.

```example
Bor du i Malmö? — Do you live in Malmö? (pitch rises at the end)
Pratar hon svenska? — Does she speak Swedish? (pitch rises at the end)
```

Try saying both versions of the same sentence out loud, one right after the
other, and exaggerate the difference — falling, then rising.

Now, back to a rule you already met: a stressed vowel is **long** before at
most one consonant, and **short** before two or more of the same consonant.
This time, apply it to words you probably haven't seen before — the rule
should work on its own, without you needing to know the word first.

```example
ful — ugly (one "l", long "u")
full — full, drunk (two "l"s, short "u")
sil — sieve (one "l", long "i")
sill — herring (two "l"s, short "i")
hal — slippery (one "l", long "a")
hall — hall, corridor (two "l"s, short "a")
vek — weak, soft (one "k", long "e")
veck — fold, crease (two "k"s, short "e")
```

Cover the translations and just look at the spelling: count the consonant
letters right after the vowel, then decide — long or short — before you check
the answer. That's the real skill this lesson is building.
