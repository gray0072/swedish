Back in sfi-a you learned "en" and "ett" as a fact about each noun — just memorised
labels. Now let's look at them as a system: they also decide how the noun forms its
**definite** singular ("the car", not just "a car").

The rule depends on two things: is the noun **en** or **ett**, and does it end in a
**consonant** or a **vowel**?

| gender | ends in consonant | ends in vowel |
|---|---|---|
| en-word | + **-en** | + **-n** |
| ett-word | + **-et** | + **-t** |

```example
en bil — bilen (consonant, en-word)
en flicka — flickan (vowel, en-word)
ett hus — huset (consonant, ett-word)
ett äpple — äpplet (vowel, ett-word)
```

More examples across both genders:

```example
en katt – katten — the cat
en dag – dagen — the day
en gata – gatan — the street
ett bord – bordet — the table
ett barn – barnet — the child
ett öga – ögat — the eye
```

Notice: an en-word ending in a consonant adds a full syllable, **-en** (bil ->
bil**en**). An en-word already ending in a vowel just adds **-n** (flicka ->
flicka**n**) — Swedish doesn't like two vowels colliding awkwardly, so only the
consonant "n" is added. The same logic applies to ett-words: consonant-ending nouns
add **-et** (hus -> hus**et**), vowel-ending nouns add just **-t** (äpple ->
äpple**t**).

So the four singular forms of every Swedish noun are: indefinite (en/ett + noun),
and definite (noun + one of -en/-n/-et/-t, chosen by gender and final sound). Once
you know a noun's gender and whether it ends in a vowel, the definite form is fully
predictable — no more guessing needed for this part.
