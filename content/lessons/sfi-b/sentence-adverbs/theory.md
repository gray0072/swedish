Swedish has a small group of adverbs — grammar calls them "satsadverbial" (sentence
adverbs) — that comment on the whole sentence rather than describe a single word:
**inte** (not), **alltid** (always), **aldrig** (never), **ofta** (often), **kanske**
(maybe), **snart** (soon), **gärna** (gladly). They all follow one simple rule in a
main clause: they go **directly after the finite verb**.

```example
Jag äter inte kött. — I don't eat meat.
Han är alltid trött. — He is always tired.
Vi äter aldrig fisk. — We never eat fish.
Vi går ofta på bio. — We often go to the cinema.
Jag kommer kanske imorgon. — I'll maybe come tomorrow.
```

This slot — right after the verb — is fixed. Swedish learners whose first language
puts "not" before the verb (like English "I do **not** eat") often want to say
*"Jag inte äter"*, but that is wrong. The finite verb always comes first, then the
sentence adverb.

```example
Bussen kommer snart. — The bus is coming soon.
Jag vill gärna hjälpa dig. — I'd gladly help you.
```

If the clause has two verbs (a modal plus an infinitive, for example), the sentence
adverb still sits right after the **finite** verb — the one that carries the tense —
not after the infinitive:

```example
Jag vill inte äta kött. — I don't want to eat meat.
```

Here **vill** is finite (present tense of "vilja"), so **inte** follows it
immediately, before the infinitive **äta**.

One important note for later: this rule — verb, then sentence adverb — applies to
**main clauses**. Subordinate clauses (after words like "att", "om", "eftersom")
follow a different order, where the adverb moves before the verb. That pattern has
its own name, BIFF, and belongs to a later lesson — for now, just master the main-
clause placement shown above.
