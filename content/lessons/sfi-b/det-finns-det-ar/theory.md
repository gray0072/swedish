Swedish has two different phrases that both translate loosely as "there is" or
"it is" in English, and mixing them up is a very common mistake. Learn them as
a pair, not one at a time.

**det finns** — "there is / there are", used when you talk about something
*existing* or being available.

```example
Det finns en park här. — There is a park here.
Det finns många sjöar i Sverige. — There are many lakes in Sweden.
Det finns inte mjölk hemma. — There is no milk at home.
```

**det är** — "it is", used when you *identify* or *describe* something that is
already there or already known.

```example
Det är en park. — It is a park.
Det är kallt idag. — It is cold today.
Det är min bror. — That is my brother.
```

Compare the two directly:

| sentence | meaning |
|---|---|
| Det finns en park här. | A park exists here (you didn't know that). |
| Det är en park. | (Pointing at something) — that thing is a park. |
| Det finns ett problem. | A problem exists. |
| Det är ett problem. | This (specific thing) is a problem. |

A simple test: if you can replace the phrase with "exists" or "is available",
use **det finns**. If you can replace it with "it is" while pointing at
something, or describing weather, mood, or a situation, use **det är**.

```example
Det finns choklad i skåpet. — There is chocolate in the cupboard.
Det är gott. — It is tasty.
```

Both phrases stay the same regardless of what follows — **finns** does not
change for singular or plural, and neither does **är**:

```example
Det finns en bil. — There is one car.
Det finns tio bilar. — There are ten cars.
```

Once you can hear the difference between "something exists" and "something is
described", these two little words will make many everyday sentences —
weather, directions, complaints — much easier to build correctly.
