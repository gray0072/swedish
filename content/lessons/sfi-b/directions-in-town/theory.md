Directions in a Swedish town reuse a small, familiar set of words. You still ask with **Ursäkta, var är ...?**, but now the answer often names something bigger than a single corner.

```example
Ursäkta, hur kommer jag till centrum? — Excuse me, how do I get to the town centre?
Är det långt till stationen? — Is it far to the station?
```

The command verb is now **sväng** ("turn"), and it's common to drop "till" and go straight to the direction: **Sväng höger vid korsningen** ("Turn right at the intersection") is just as natural as the fuller **Sväng till höger**. A **korsning** is where two streets meet; a **rondell** is a roundabout — you drive around it and pick an exit (**avfart**) by counting: first, second, third.

Distance and position get their own short words: **nära** ("near"), **långt** ("far"), and **mittemot** ("opposite") — handy for landmarks, as in **Affären ligger mittemot torget** ("The shop is opposite the square").

```example
Kör runt rondellen och ta andra avfarten. — Go around the roundabout and take the second exit.
Gå över vid övergångsstället. — Cross at the pedestrian crossing.
```

For people on foot, three words matter: a **gångbana** (sidewalk) is for walking, an **övergångsställe** (pedestrian crossing) is where you cross safely, and a **hållplats** (bus stop) is where you wait for the bus. If someone walks past without stopping, they **går förbi**.

When you're lost, the simplest move is to **fråga om vägen** ("ask for directions") — Swedes are usually happy to help, and showing your **karta** (map) or phone while asking makes the answer much easier to follow. If you still can't **hitta** ("find") the place, don't hesitate to ask again — a slower, second explanation is completely normal.
