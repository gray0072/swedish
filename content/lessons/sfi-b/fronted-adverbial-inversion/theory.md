You already know the V2 rule: the finite verb sits in second position in a Swedish
main clause. Usually the subject comes first, so it looks like "subject-verb":
**jag jobbar** ("I work"). But the first position doesn't have to hold the subject —
it can hold *anything*, including a time, place, or manner adverbial. When that
happens, the verb still has to stay in second position, so the subject is pushed
after the verb. This swap is called **inversion**.

```example
Jag jobbar på måndag. — I work on Monday.
På måndag jobbar jag. — On Monday, I work.
```

Compare the plain, subject-first order with the fronted, inverted order:

```example
Många människor bor i Sverige. — Many people live in Sweden.
I Sverige bor många människor. — In Sweden, many people live.

Det är kallt idag. — It's cold today.
Idag är det kallt. — Today, it's cold.

Jag lagar mat ibland. — I cook food sometimes.
Ibland lagar jag mat. — Sometimes I cook food.
```

Notice the pattern in each pair: whatever comes first (the fronted word or phrase),
the finite verb comes right after it — never the subject. Swedes front adverbials
like this constantly, especially time expressions, to put emphasis on them or to
connect a sentence smoothly to what was just said.

A simple way to check yourself: find the finite verb, and make sure exactly one
"slot" of information sits in front of it. If the subject is in that slot, order is
normal (subject-verb). If something else is in that slot, the subject must move
after the verb.

```example
Sedan går vi hem. — Then we go home.
Där ligger biblioteket. — There lies the library.
```

This is not a special exception — it is the same V2 rule you already know, just
applied when the first position is filled by something other than the subject. Get
comfortable with it, because fronted adverbials are everywhere in natural Swedish,
from weather talk to storytelling.
