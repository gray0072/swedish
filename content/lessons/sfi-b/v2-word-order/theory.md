You already know that in a Swedish statement, if you start with a time word,
the verb comes right before "jag": *Idag läser jag en bok.* Now it's time to
state the full rule behind that pattern, called **V2** ("verb second"): in a
Swedish main clause, the finite (conjugated) verb is always the **second**
element — no matter what the **first** element is.

The first slot can be filled by almost anything: the subject, a time word, an
object, a place — even a whole phrase. Whatever goes there, the verb follows
immediately after it, and everything else (including the subject, if it
wasn't first) comes after the verb.

```example
Jag läser en bok. — I read a book. (subject first)
Idag läser jag en bok. — Today I'm reading a book. (time word first)
En bok läser jag inte. — A book, I don't read. (object first, for emphasis)
```

Look closely at the third example: **en bok** (the object) is moved to the
front to emphasize it, and the verb **läser** still comes right after it — the
subject **jag** simply moves to third position. This shows V2 is not "verb
after subject" — it's "verb after whatever comes first."

More examples with different first elements:

```example
Ofta läser jag på kvällen. — I often read in the evening.
Tyvärr kan jag inte komma. — Unfortunately, I can't come.
Där bor min syster. — There lives my sister.
```

A useful way to check yourself: find the finite verb, count backwards — there
should be exactly one element before it. If there are two (like *Idag jag
läser*), the order is wrong; move the verb to second place: *Idag läser jag*.

This rule holds for statements; questions and subordinate clauses have their
own patterns, covered separately.
