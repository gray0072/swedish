Swedish money is called **pengar** — a word that only exists in the plural; there
is no singular form for "money" itself. The currency is the **krona** (plural
**kronor**), abbreviated **kr** on price tags and **SEK** in banks. Physical
money comes in two forms: **mynt** (coins) and **sedlar** (banknotes, singular
**en sedel**). Since 2010 Sweden no longer mints öre coins, so the smallest
coin you'll actually meet is **1 krona** — anything below that survives only
on paper, in prices and receipts, not in your **plånbok** (wallet).

```example
en krona — one krona (coin)
en sedel — a banknote
ett mynt — a coin
en plånbok — a wallet
```

To ask a price, use **Vad kostar det?** or **Hur mycket kostar det?** — "How
much does it cost?" The thing itself is the **pris** (price); if it's reduced,
that's a **rabatt** (discount); and if it costs nothing, it's **gratis**
(free) — an adjective that never changes form, whatever the gender or number.

```example
Vad kostar biljetten? — How much does the ticket cost?
Den kostar tvåhundra kronor. — It costs two hundred kronor.
Finns det någon rabatt? — Is there a discount?
```

As for paying, Swedes rarely reach for cash at all. You can still **betala
kontant** (pay in cash), but far more common are **betala med kort** (pay by
card) and, especially between friends, **swisha** — to send money instantly
through the Swish mobile app. It's now an ordinary verb in everyday Swedish,
the way "to google" is in English.

A few more useful expressions: **spara pengar** (to save money), **låna
pengar** (to borrow money — note that **låna** also means "to lend," so a
preposition tells you which: **låna av** someone is to borrow from them,
**låna till** someone is to lend to them), and **växla pengar** (to exchange
money, for example at a currency exchange office). And if a cashier hands
money back to you, that's your **växel** — your change.
