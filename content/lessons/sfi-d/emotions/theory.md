In Swedish, people often ask **hur känner du dig?** (how do you feel?) when they want to talk about **känslor** (feelings). You answer with the verb **känna sig** (to feel) plus an adjective: **glad** (happy), **ledsen** (sad), **arg** (angry), or **trött** (tired).

```example
Hur känner du dig idag? — How do you feel today?
Jag känner mig glad och lugn. — I feel happy and calm.
```

Sometimes you feel **orolig** (worried) before something important, or **nervös** (nervous) before an exam. You can also become **förvånad** (surprised) by news, **stolt** (proud) of something you've done, or **rädd** (scared) in the dark. The opposite of worry and fear is **lugn** (calm).

```example
Jag är lite orolig inför intervjun. — I'm a bit worried about the interview.
Hon blev förvånad och glad på samma gång. — She was surprised and happy at the same time.
```

Strong feelings often show in action: you can **gråta** (cry) from **glädje** (joy) or sorrow, and **skratta** (laugh) when something is funny. It's completely normal for feelings to change over the course of a day — what matters is being able to put them into words.

```example
Han skrattade högt åt skämtet. — He laughed loudly at the joke.
Hon grät av glädje när hon fick jobbet. — She cried with joy when she got the job.
```
