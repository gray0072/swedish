You already know word accent 1 and 2 from a single word. In a real sentence
at natural speed, two more layers stack on top of that: **satsbetoning**
(sentence stress) and **reduktion** (reduction) of the words around it.

## Sentence stress: not every word is equal

Content words (nouns, verbs, adjectives, question words) carry stress; small
grammatical words (pronouns, articles, auxiliary verbs, conjunctions) mostly
don't. The word with the newest or most important information — the
**fokusord** — gets the strongest stress of all.

```example
Jag HAR sett FILMEN. — I've seen the FILM. (fokusord: filmen)
Jag har INTE sett filmen. — I have NOT seen the film. (fokusord: inte)
```

Moving the stress changes what's being emphasised, even with the same words.

## Reduction: small words shrink

Frequent, unstressed function words are shortened or weakened in fast,
connected speech — this is completely normal, not sloppy pronunciation.

```example
Jag har sett det. → Jag 'ar sett de. — I've seen it. (har loses its h; det sounds like "de")
Fisk och skaldjur. → Fisk å skaldjur. — Fish and seafood. (och → å)
De sa att de skulle komma. → Dom sa att dom skulle komma. — They said they'd come. (de → dom)
```

None of this shows up in writing — you write **har**, **och**, **de** — but
recognising the reduced spoken forms is essential for understanding Swedes
speaking at a normal pace, not the careful, word-by-word pace of early
lessons.

## Rhythm across the whole sentence

Put together, Swedish sentence rhythm alternates: a stressed content word,
then a cluster of quick, reduced little words, then the next stressed word —
giving spoken Swedish its characteristic "melodic" flow rather than an
even, syllable-by-syllable beat.

## In short

Listen for the stressed **fokusord** to catch a sentence's main point, and
don't be thrown by small words shrinking around it — **har**, **och**,
**det** rarely sound the way they're spelled once speech speeds up.
