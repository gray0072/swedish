In written, often literary style, a subordinate clause of time or manner can be
compressed into a short participial phrase. This makes the sentence more compact and
gives it a literary feel.

## The present participle in place of a subordinate clause

The present participle (ending in `-ande`/`-ende`, invariable for gender and number) can
stand at the start or in the middle of a sentence, replacing a whole subordinate clause
with `när`/`medan`.

```example
Leende gick hon in i rummet. — Smiling, she entered the room.
Hon log när hon gick in i rummet. — She smiled as she entered the room. (the full clause)
```

Both sentences describe the same scene, but the participial phrase is shorter and reads
more literary — it belongs to narrative style, not everyday speech.

## Another example: the compressed and full forms side by side

```example
Visslande gick han längs gatan. — Whistling, he walked along the street.
Han visslade medan han gick längs gatan. — He whistled while he walked along the street. (the full clause)
```

## How to unpack a participial phrase back into a clause

| Participial phrase | Full subordinate clause |
|---|---|
| Leende gick hon in. | Hon log när hon gick in. |
| Visslande gick han längs gatan. | Han visslade medan han gick längs gatan. |
| Skrattande lämnade barnen skolan. | Barnen skrattade när de lämnade skolan. |

The participle's action almost always happens **at the same time** as the main verb's
action — matching the meaning of `när`/`medan`, not "after" or "before."

## Where this fits

Participial phrases are typical of literary prose, news reports, and descriptive
writing — anywhere fluency and economy matter. In everyday spoken language, a full
clause with `när` or `medan` is used far more often.

## In short

A present participle at the start or in the middle of a sentence is a compressed
version of a time/manner clause; it never changes form and expresses an action
simultaneous with the main verb's.
