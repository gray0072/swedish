You already know *where* **inte** goes — after the finite verb in a main
clause, before it in a subordinate clause (the BIFF rule). Now look at *what
exactly* it negates — its **räckvidd** (scope) — because the same sentence
can carry two different meanings depending on where the negation sits.

## Same words, different scope

```example
Alla kom inte. — Not everyone came. (some did, some didn't — partial
negation)
Inte alla kom. — Not everyone came. (fronted for emphasis: the claim
"everyone came" is flatly rejected)
```

Both translate similarly into English, but moving **inte** next to **alla**
sharpens the focus onto that one word — a stylistic and rhetorical choice,
common in more careful or argumentative writing.

## Beyond inte: a small family of negators

**Ingen/inget/inga** is a negative determiner — it replaces "en/ett/plural +
noun" and stands *in place of* inte, not alongside it.

```example
Jag har inget svar. — I have no answer. (not: *jag har inte ett svar*)
Ingen kom. — No one came.
```

**Aldrig** ("never") and **knappast** ("hardly, scarcely") both slot into
the same sentence-adverb position as **inte**, but express different shades
of negation — total absence, and a doubtful, weak denial.

```example
Han svarar aldrig i tid. — He never answers on time. (categorical)
Det är knappast rätt svar. — That's hardly the right answer. (a soft doubt,
not a flat denial)
```

## Scope in a subordinate clause

The BIFF placement — **inte** before the finite verb — holds for the whole
family of negators in a subordinate clause too.

```example
Hon sa att hon aldrig hade sett filmen. — She said she'd never seen the
film.
```

## In short

Negation isn't just "yes or no" — its exact position decides whether the
whole clause is denied or just one part of it, and the choice between
**inte**, **ingen**, **aldrig** and **knappast** adds a further shade of
certainty or doubt.
