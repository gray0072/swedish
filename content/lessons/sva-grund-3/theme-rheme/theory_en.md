Every sentence carries two kinds of information: the **theme** (what the sentence is
about — already known, given) and the **rheme** (the new thing being said about the
theme). In Swedish V2, the theme usually occupies the fundament (first position),
while the rheme unfolds later, in the huvuddel. But which word actually goes into the
fundament is a choice — and it determines what the sentence is "really" about.

## Same facts, different fundament

Take one fact: yesterday Anna bought a book. The same content can be presented in at
least three ways:

| Fundament (theme) | Sentence | What the sentence is about |
|---|---|---|
| Anna | Anna köpte boken i går. | Anna: what she did |
| Boken | Boken köpte Anna i går. | the book: who bought it and when |
| I går | I går köpte Anna boken. | yesterday: what happened then |

```example
Anna köpte boken i går. — Anna bought the book yesterday.
Boken köpte Anna i går. — That book, Anna bought it yesterday.
I går köpte Anna boken. — Yesterday, Anna bought the book.
```

All three sentences are grammatically correct — what differs is not the facts
themselves, but what is presented as already known (the theme) versus the main news
(the rheme).

## The fundament keeps a text coherent

Choosing the fundament is not arbitrary — it's a tool for text cohesion: the
fundament usually carries something already mentioned in the previous sentence.

```example
Vi hittade en gammal bok på vinden. Boken visade sig vara mycket värdefull. — We found an old book in the attic. The book turned out to be very valuable.
```

The second sentence opens with "Boken" — not because grammar requires it, but
because "the book" is already known from the first sentence: the theme of the second
sentence picks up the rheme of the first. If the second sentence instead opened with
something new ("I går visade det sig..."), the link between the two sentences would
be far less obvious.

## A practical rule of thumb

When writing a connected text (a referat, an essay), check that each sentence's
fundament usually builds on something the reader already knows — from the previous
sentence or from shared context — while new information (the rheme) is saved for the
end. This isn't a strict rule but a stylistic principle that makes a text more
cohesive and easier to read.
