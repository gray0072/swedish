To talk about a whole category — not one specific dog, but dogs in general —
Swedish has three different noun forms, and they carry slightly different
flavours.

## Three ways to say the same general thing

```example
En hund är trogen. — A dog is loyal. (indefinite singular)
Hunden är ett troget djur. — The dog is a loyal animal. (definite singular)
Hundar är trogna. — Dogs are loyal. (bare plural, no article)
```

All three describe dogs as a category, not a particular animal — despite
looking, on the surface, like sentences about "a dog" or "the dog".

## When each form fits best

The **bare plural** (*hundar*, no article) is by far the most common,
everyday way to make a general statement — it's the default choice in
speech and casual writing.

```example
Björnar sover på vintern. — Bears sleep in winter.
```

The **definite singular** (*hunden*) sounds more formal or scientific — as
if defining the species as a type, the way an encyclopedia or textbook
would.

```example
Hunden är ett troget djur. — The dog is a loyal animal. (a definition,
almost encyclopedic in tone)
```

The **indefinite singular** (*en hund*) picks out one representative member
to stand for the whole category — useful when making a point about what any
single dog is like.

```example
En hund behöver rörelse varje dag. — A dog needs exercise every day. (true
of any dog you pick)
```

## Mass nouns skip the article entirely

Uncountable nouns like *vatten* (water) use no article at all in a generic
statement, singular or plural.

```example
Människor behöver vatten. — Human beings need water.
```

## In short

Choose the bare plural for an ordinary general statement, the definite
singular for a formal, definition-like one, and the indefinite singular when
you mean "any single member of the category."
