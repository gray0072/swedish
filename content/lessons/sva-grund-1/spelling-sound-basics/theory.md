You've met vowel length before, spread across earlier lessons. Now
systematise it into one clear spelling rule that predicts almost every
double consonant you'll see in Swedish.

## The core rule

In a stressed syllable: a **long vowel** is followed by a **single**
consonant letter; a **short vowel** is followed by a **doubled** consonant
letter.

```example
vit — white (long i, one t)
vitt — white, ett-form (short i, double t)
mat — food (long a, one t)
matt — matte, dull (short a, double t)
```

Same letters, same word family in some cases, completely different sound
and meaning — spelled entirely by this one rule.

## Verbs follow the same logic

```example
sila — to strain (long i, one l): silar, silade, silat
sitta — to sit (short i, double t): sitter, satt, suttit
```

## Why it matters for reading and writing

Once you know the rule, you can predict a new word's pronunciation just
from its spelling — a single consonant after a vowel signals a long vowel
sound, a doubled consonant signals a short one. It also tells you how to
*spell* a word you've only heard: if the vowel sounded short, double the
following consonant.

## A few exceptions

A handful of common words end in a single consonant after a short vowel —
**hund** (dog), **han** (he), **kan** (can) among them. These don't follow
the doubling rule at the very end of a word and must simply be learned as
exceptions; the rule is strongest in the middle of a word, before another
syllable.

```example
Hunden silar mjölken genom en duk. — The dog strains the milk through a
cloth. (hund: short vowel, single consonant — an exception; silar: long
vowel, single consonant — the regular rule)
```

## In short

Long vowel → one consonant letter; short vowel → doubled consonant letter —
this single rule predicts spelling and pronunciation together for the vast
majority of Swedish words.
