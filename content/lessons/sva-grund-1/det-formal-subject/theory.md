Every Swedish main clause needs a subject — the V2 rule demands it. When
there's no real actor to put there, Swedish reaches for **det** as a
**formellt subjekt** (formal subject), a **platshållare** (placeholder) with
no meaning of its own.

## Existence: det finns

To say something exists or is present somewhere, without naming it as the
grammatical subject, use **det finns**.

```example
Det finns en park här. — There is a park here.
Det finns inga biljetter kvar. — There are no tickets left.
```

The real, notional subject — *en park*, *inga biljetter* — sits after the
verb; **det** just occupies the slot the V2 rule needs filled.

## Identifying or describing: det är

**Det är** points at or describes something, often something already
mentioned or right in front of you.

```example
Det är en bra bok. — It is a good book.
Det är viktigt att äta frukost. — It is important to eat breakfast.
```

## Weather: det regnar, det snöar

Weather verbs have no logical subject at all — nothing "does" the raining —
so **det** is required simply to satisfy the grammar.

```example
Det regnar mycket idag. — It's raining a lot today.
Det snöar i norra Sverige. — It's snowing in northern Sweden.
Det är kallt ute idag. — It's cold outside today.
```

## Formal versus real subject

Compare a sentence with a real subject to one using formal **det** for the
same idea:

```example
En park ligger här. — A park is located here. (real subject: en park)
Det finns en park här. — There is a park here. (formal subject: det)
```

## In short

Whenever a clause needs a subject but has nothing meaningful to put there —
existence, weather, or a general description — Swedish fills the slot with
an empty **det**, not because it refers to anything, but because the
sentence's grammar requires a subject.
