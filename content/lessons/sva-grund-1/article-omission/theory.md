You already know **en/ett** well from ordinary nouns — *en bok, ett hus*.
Three situations drop the article entirely, and it's worth learning them as
a set.

## Profession after är

When you say what someone's job is, using **är** + a profession, Swedish
leaves out the article that English or Russian would use.

```example
Hon är lärare. — She is a teacher. (not "en lärare")
Han är läkare. — He is a doctor. (not "en läkare")
```

The article comes back if the profession is described with an adjective:
*Hon är en duktig lärare* ("She is a good teacher") — the article returns
once the noun phrase grows.

## Mass nouns (ämnesnamn)

Uncountable nouns — food, drink, material — take no article when they refer
to the substance in general, not a specific portion.

```example
Jag dricker vatten varje dag. — I drink water every day.
Vi äter bröd till frukost. — We eat bread for breakfast.
```

## Fixed phrases

A handful of common expressions simply lost their article over time and
must be learned as whole chunks.

```example
Barnen går i skolan. — The children attend school. (a fixed phrase about
attending, not a specific building)
Han har feber idag. — He has a fever today.
Jag brukar ta buss till jobbet. — I usually take the bus to work. (generic
mode of transport)
```

Compare the last one with the article present: **ta bussen** means boarding
one particular, identifiable bus, while **ta buss** just names the means of
transport.

## In short

Drop the article after **är + profession**, with mass nouns naming a
substance in general, and inside a handful of fixed everyday phrases — but
the article returns as soon as the noun is made specific or described.
