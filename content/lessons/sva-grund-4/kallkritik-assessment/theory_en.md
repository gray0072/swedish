When analysing a source, the Swedish school tradition uses four classic **källkritik** (source-criticism) criteria. The first is **äkthet** (authenticity): is the source really what it claims to be, and has it not been forged?

```example
Man måste alltid kontrollera källans äkthet innan man litar på den. — You must always check a source's authenticity before trusting it.
```

The second criterion is **tidssamband** (time relevance): the closer a source was created to the event itself, the more reliable it usually is. A memory recalled twenty years later is less accurate than a note made at the time of the event.

The third criterion is **beroende** (dependency): is the source independent, or does it simply copy a **primärkälla** (primary source) without checking it, becoming a **sekundärkälla** (secondary source) with added errors?

```example
Källan verkar ha ett beroende av en annan artikel utan att ange varifrån informationen kommer. — The source seems to have a dependency on another article without stating where the information comes from.
```

The fourth criterion is **tendens** (bias): does the **avsändare** (sender, author) have a hidden **syfte** (purpose) — for example, to sell something or to influence the reader's opinion — that could make the source **vinklad** (slanted) rather than **opartisk** (impartial)?

```example
Källan verkar ha en tydlig tendens eftersom avsändaren vill sälja en produkt. — The source seems to have a clear bias, since the sender wants to sell a product.
```

When writing a **referat** (a neutral summary of someone else's text) for a source analysis, it is important not to mix it up with your own opinion. It is also worth **verifiera** (verifying) facts across several sources so you are not caught out by **desinformation** (disinformation), and to weigh the overall **trovärdighet** (credibility) of a text.
