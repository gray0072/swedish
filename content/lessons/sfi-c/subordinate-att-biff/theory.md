The word **att** ("that") introduces a subordinate clause — a clause with its
own subject and verb, attached to a main clause. It works like English "that",
except Swedish never drops it the way English drops "that" in casual speech.

```example
Jag tror att han kommer. — I think that he is coming.
Hon sa att hon var trött. — She said that she was tired.
```

Word order changes inside a subordinate clause. In a **main clause**, a
sentence adverb like **inte** ("not") comes right after the finite verb:

```example
Jag vet inte. — I don't know.
Han kommer inte. — He isn't coming.
```

But inside a **subordinate clause** — after att, and also after om, när,
eftersom, etc. — inte moves to stand **before** the finite verb instead. This
is often taught as the **BIFF** rule: **B**isats (subordinate clause),
**I**nte, **F**inita verbet (finite verb), **F**örst (comes first) — i.e. in a
bisats, inte comes before the finite verb.

```example
Jag sa att jag inte visste. — I said that I didn't know.
Han tror att hon inte kommer. — He thinks that she isn't coming.
```

Compare the two patterns side by side:

| clause type | order | example |
|---|---|---|
| main clause | verb — inte | Jag vet inte. |
| subordinate clause | inte — verb | ...att jag inte visste. |

The same shift happens with other sentence adverbs (aldrig "never", alltid
"always") and after any subordinating word, not only att. A useful check: if
you can insert "that" in the English translation, you are in a subordinate
clause, and inte must move before the verb.

```example
Jag hoppas att det inte regnar. — I hope that it doesn't rain.
Vi tycker att maten inte är dyr. — We think that the food isn't expensive.
```

Remember: the subject and finite verb are still present in the subordinate
clause — att does not turn the verb into an infinitive. "Jag tror att han
kommer" keeps "kommer" as a normal present-tense verb, just like in a main
clause; only the position of inte changes.
