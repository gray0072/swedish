Winter in Sweden means something specific: not just cold, but **mörker** (darkness) that arrives in the afternoon and a constant risk of **halka** (black ice) on every sidewalk. The days grow short — it can be **mörkt** by four o'clock — so many Swedes wear a **reflex** (reflector) when walking or cycling so drivers can see them.

```example
Det är halka på trottoaren. — There's black ice on the sidewalk.
Jag halkade och föll. — I slipped and fell.
```

To handle the streets safely, people attach **halkskydd** (ice grips) to their shoes, and by law, cars must switch to **vinterdäck** (winter tires) once winter road conditions begin. **Snö** (snow) and **is** (ice) cover everything, and it can **snöa** for days, sometimes building into a **snöstorm**.

```example
Man måste byta till vinterdäck i november. — You have to switch to winter tires in November.
Jag fryser om händerna. — My hands are freezing.
```

Dressing right matters as much as driving right. **Varma kläder** (warm clothes) usually means a warm coat, a **mössa** (beanie) that covers the ears, and a pair of **vantar** (mittens) — thin gloves rarely feel warm enough once it's several **minusgrader** (degrees below zero). If snow piles up outside your door, you'll need to **skotta** (shovel) before you can even get out.

```example
Ta på dig en varm mössa och vantar. — Put on a warm hat and mittens.
Vi måste skotta trappan. — We have to shovel the steps.
```

Swedes rarely let winter stop daily life — schools, buses, and workplaces keep running through snow and darkness. Knowing this handful of words about **kyla** (cold), **halka**, and dressing warmly will help you talk about the season and stay safe on icy streets.
