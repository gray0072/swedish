**Man** is one of the most useful little words in Swedish — a subject
pronoun meaning "one", "you (in general)", or "people in general", used when
you're not talking about a specific person.

```example
Man får inte röka här. — You're not allowed to smoke here.
Man får vänta i kön. — One has to wait in the queue.
```

It behaves like any subject pronoun and has its own object and possessive
forms: **en** (object) and **ens** (possessive) — not "man" or "mans".

```example
Det gör en glad. — That makes you (one) happy.
Det gör en glad att träffa vänner. — Meeting friends makes one happy.
```

**Ens** shows up especially in reflexive-feeling statements about "one's
own" something:

```example
Det är viktigt att ta hand om sin egen hälsa och lyssna på ens kropp. — It's important to take care of one's own health and listen to one's body.
Man ska lyssna på sin kropp. — One should listen to one's body.
```

Don't confuse this pronoun with the completely unrelated noun **man**
meaning "husband" or "man" — spelled the same, but a different word,
disambiguated entirely by context and by grammar (the noun takes an article:
en man, a man/husband).

```example
Hon har en man och två barn. — She has a husband and two children.
```

Compare the pronoun and the noun side by side:

```example
Man får inte röka här. — You're not allowed to smoke here. (pronoun: people in general)
Hon har en man. — She has a husband. (noun: a person)
```

"Man" is common in signs, instructions and general statements about rules or
habits — you'll meet it constantly once you start reading real Swedish text.
