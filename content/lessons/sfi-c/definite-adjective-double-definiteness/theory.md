You already know that a noun marks definiteness with its own ending: **en
stad** → **staden** (the city). When an adjective steps in front of that
definite noun, Swedish adds a *second* marker of definiteness — a free-standing
article — so the phrase ends up definite twice over. This is called **double
definiteness**.

```example
Den stora staden. — The big city.
Det gamla huset. — The old house.
De nya bilarna. — The new cars.
```

The article depends on the noun's gender and number, exactly like the
definite ending itself: **den** for en-words, **det** for ett-words, **de**
for the plural.

| gender | article | noun (definite) | adjective |
|---|---|---|---|
| en | den | staden | stora |
| ett | det | huset | gamla |
| plural | de | bilarna | nya |

Notice that in all three rows the adjective ends in **-a** — this is its
*weak* form, used whenever the adjective sits before a definite noun,
regardless of the noun's gender or number. Compare with the ordinary base
form used without an article:

```example
en stor stad — a big city (indefinite, base form: stor)
den stora staden — the big city (definite, weak form: stora)
```

More examples, to fix the pattern:

```example
Den vackra staden. — The beautiful city.
De nya bilarna kostar mycket. — The new cars cost a lot.
```

One exception you'll meet later: after **min/din/hans** and similar
possessives, the double article disappears and only the weak adjective
remains ("min stora bil"), but that belongs to a later lesson.
