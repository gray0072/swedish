Swedish uses pitch not just for whole sentences (statement vs question, from
kurs B) but on individual words, too. Every stressed syllable carries one of
two pitch patterns, called **word accents**.

**Accent 1** (acute) is a single, simple pitch peak — common in short words
and most one-syllable words.

**Accent 2** (grave) is a double, rise-fall-rise pattern spread over two
syllables — common in most two-syllable words and almost all compounds.

The same spelling can carry either accent, giving two completely different
words:

```example
anden (accent 1) — the duck
anden (accent 2) — the spirit
```

```example
tomten (accent 1) — the plot (of land)
tomten (accent 2) — the house gnome / Santa
```

You won't always be able to tell these apart from context alone, so it's
worth training your ear on a few classic minimal pairs like these — but don't
worry if it takes time; even careful learners often lean on context (as
Swedes themselves sometimes do too).

The word accent doesn't disappear in a full sentence — it's the foundation
of the overall **melodi** (melody/intonation) that stretches across several
stressed words, rising and falling as the sentence goes on.

```example
Anden simmar i sjön. — The duck swims in the lake.
Tomten kommer på julafton. — Santa comes on Christmas Eve.
```

A useful way to notice this: listen to a longer Swedish sentence and try to
hear each stressed syllable's little rise or fall — that's the accent 1/2
pattern of individual words, chained together into the sentence's overall
tune.
