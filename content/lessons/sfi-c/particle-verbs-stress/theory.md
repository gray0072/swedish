Swedish has many **particle verbs** (*partikelverb*): a verb plus a small
adverb — the particle — that together create a new meaning. The particle is
not just decoration; drop it and the meaning changes completely.

```example
ringa — to call (phone)
ringa upp — to call back / call up
```

The single most important fact about particle verbs is this: **the stress
falls on the particle, not on the verb.** Say it out loud: *jag ringer
UPP dig*, not *jag RINGer upp dig*. The particle carries the melody of the
phrase.

```example
Jag ringer UPP dig imorgon. — I'll call you back tomorrow.
Han tar AV sig jackan. — He takes off his jacket.
Jag kommer inte iHÅG hans namn. — I don't remember his name.
Kan du fylla I blanketten? — Can you fill in the form?
```

This stress pattern is more than pronunciation — it can distinguish a
particle verb (new meaning) from a plain verb followed by a preposition
(literal meaning). Compare:

```example
Hon går UPP för trappan. — She walks up the stairs. (particle verb: "goes upstairs")
Hon går upp FÖR trappan. — She walks in front of the stairs. (verb + preposition, literal)
```

In the first sentence, *upp* is stressed and the phrase means "goes
upstairs." In everyday speech this distinction rarely causes real confusion,
but it explains why Swedes stress particles so clearly — the melody itself
carries meaning.

A handful of frequent particle verbs to learn as fixed chunks:

```example
Eleven skriver AV texten. — The student copies the text.
Glöm inte att stänga AV datorn. — Don't forget to switch off the computer.
Tåget kommer FRAM klockan sju. — The train arrives at seven.
Han ger aldrig UPP. — He never gives up.
```

Best strategy: learn each particle verb as one unit, with its stress, rather
than translating the verb and particle separately — many combinations are not
predictable from the parts alone.
