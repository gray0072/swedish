Swedish has two different ways to say "his", "her" or "their" — and mixing them up
changes who owns what.

**sin / sitt / sina** refer back to the **subject of the same clause**. They mean
"his own", "her own", "their own" — the owner is the very person doing the action.

```example
Anna läser sin bok. — Anna is reading her (own) book.
Erik läser sitt brev. — Erik is reading his (own) letter.
De läser sina böcker. — They are reading their (own) books.
```

**hans / hennes / deras** point to someone **else's** possession — not the subject of
the clause. They never change form for en/ett/plural.

```example
Anna läser hans bok. — Anna is reading his (someone else's) book.
Erik läser hennes brev. — Erik is reading her (someone else's) letter.
Vi läser deras böcker. — We are reading their (other people's) books.
```

Compare the minimal pair directly — same subject, same verb, only the possessive
changes:

```example
Anna läser sin bok. — Anna is reading her own book.
Anna läser hans bok. — Anna is reading his (someone else's) book.
```

In the first sentence, the book belongs to Anna herself. In the second, it belongs to
a different person (a man), and Anna is simply reading it. Without this distinction,
Swedish couldn't tell the two situations apart — English relies on context, but
Swedish marks it directly in the pronoun.

A quick rule of thumb: ask "who is doing the action, and who owns the thing?" If they
are the same person, use sin/sitt/sina. If they are different people, use
hans/hennes/deras (or dess, deras, and so on) instead.

```example
Han älskar sin syster. — He loves his own sister.
Maria kör sin bil, inte hans bil. — Maria drives her own car, not his car.
```

Note: sin/sitt/sina can only be used inside the same clause, and never as the
subject of a sentence — you would never say "Sin läser en bok."
