You already know **i** and **på** as static place prepositions ("in" and
"on/at"). Now let's add their moving counterpart: **till**, which always
signals direction — movement *toward* a place, never location.

```example
Jag är i skolan. — I am in school. (static — I'm already there)
Jag går till skolan. — I go to school. (direction — movement toward it)
```

The same contrast works with **på**:

```example
Jag är på jobbet. — I am at work. (static)
Hon åker till jobbet klockan åtta. — She goes to work at eight. (direction)
```

So the pattern is: **i / på** describe *where something is*, while **till**
describes *where something is going*. The static preposition (i or på)
doesn't disappear — it's simply replaced by till whenever movement is meant.

A third preposition, **hos**, means "at someone's home or place" — it's used
for people, not for buildings or places in general.

```example
Jag är hos min vän. — I am at my friend's place.
Jag är hos min syster. — I am at my sister's.
```

The important exception: **hos has no direction counterpart.** You cannot
say *"jag går hos min vän"* to mean going toward a friend. For direction
toward a person, Swedish switches to **till**:

```example
Jag ska till min vän ikväll. — I'm going to my friend's tonight.
Jag ska till läkaren imorgon. — I'm going to the doctor tomorrow.
```

So remember the three-way system:

| static | direction |
|---|---|
| i skolan, på jobbet | till skolan, till jobbet |
| hos min vän | till min vän |

A quick test: if the sentence answers "where are you?", use i/på/hos. If it
answers "where are you going?", use till.
