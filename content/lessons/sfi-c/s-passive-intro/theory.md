Walk around any Swedish town and you will see verbs ending in **-s** on signs,
doors, and notices. This is the **-s passive**, and at this stage you only need
to *recognize* it, not build it freely — the full grammar comes later.

The pattern is simple: take a verb, add **-s**, and the sentence now describes
something that *happens* or *is done*, without saying who does it.

```example
Rökning förbjuds. — Smoking is forbidden.
Dörren öppnas automatiskt. — The door opens automatically.
Här talas svenska. — Swedish is spoken here.
```

Compare it to the normal, active sentence, where someone clearly does the
action:

```example
Personalen förbjuder rökning här. — The staff forbid smoking here.
Rökning förbjuds här. — Smoking is forbidden here.
```

In the first sentence, **personalen** (the staff) is the subject doing the
forbidding. In the second, there is no actor at all — the rule itself is the
point, not who made it. That is exactly why signs and instructions love this
form: "Rökning förbjuds" sounds neutral and official, not like a personal
order from anybody.

You will meet this **-s** ending constantly in public spaces:

```example
Biljetter säljs vid ingången. — Tickets are sold at the entrance.
Personal sökes. — Staff wanted.
Frukost serveras klockan sju. — Breakfast is served at seven.
```

A quick way to recognize it: if a plain verb like **öppna** (to open),
**sälja** (to sell), or **servera** (to serve) suddenly has an extra **-s** on
the end and no subject is doing the action to anyone, you are looking at this
passive.

Some old-fashioned job ads use **-es** instead of **-s** (as in "sökes"), but
the idea is the same: something is wanted, with nobody named as the one
wanting it.

For now, just train your eye: **verb + s = something happens, and we are not
told by whom.** Building your own passive sentences, with the full range of
tenses and structures, is a topic for a later, more advanced lesson.
