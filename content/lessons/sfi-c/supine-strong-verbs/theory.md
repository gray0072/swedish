Every Swedish verb has a fourth principal part beyond infinitive, present
and preterite: the **supine**. You use it together with **har** or **hade**
to build the perfect and pluperfect tenses — the same way English uses "has
written" or "had written".

For weak (regular) verbs, the supine is easy: it looks almost like the
preterite.

```example
Jag arbetar. – Jag arbetade. – Jag har arbetat. — I work. – I worked. – I have worked.
```

For strong verbs the supine usually ends in **-it**, but the stem vowel is
often different from both the present and the preterite — so each one has
to be learned as its own three-part chain.

```example
skriva – skrev – skrivit — to write – wrote – written
äta – åt – ätit — to eat – ate – eaten
```

Here is a table of common strong verbs with all three forms:

| infinitive | preterite | supine | translation |
|---|---|---|---|
| skriva | skrev | skrivit | to write |
| äta | åt | ätit | to eat |
| dricka | drack | druckit | to drink |
| springa | sprang | sprungit | to run |
| sova | sov | sovit | to sleep |
| ta | tog | tagit | to take |
| se | såg | sett | to see |

Notice how each verb can have three completely different-looking forms:
**dricka – drack – druckit**, **ta – tog – tagit**, **se – såg – sett**. There
is no shortcut — native speakers simply know these chains, and the way to
learn them is the same way you learn any set of irregular forms: repetition
in real sentences.

```example
Har du druckit kaffe idag? — Have you drunk coffee today?
Hon har tagit bussen till jobbet. — She has taken the bus to work.
```

Once you know a verb's supine, you are ready for the next lesson: how the
perfect tense (har + supine) is actually used, and how it differs in
meaning from the preterite you already know.
