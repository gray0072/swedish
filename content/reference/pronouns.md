# Pronouns

Swedish pronouns keep the subject/object distinction Russian and English both have, add a
possessive that changes by gender the way an adjective does, and include one genuinely tricky
pair: the reflexive possessive.

## Personal and object pronouns

| Subject | Object | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| jag | mig | I / me |
| du | dig | you (sg.) |
| han | honom | he / him |
| hon | henne | she / her |
| den / det | den / det | it (en-word / ett-word) |
| vi | oss | we / us |
| ni | er | you (pl.) |
| de | dem | they / them |

```example
Jag ser honom. — I see him.
Han ser mig. — He sees me.
```

## Possessive pronouns agree like adjectives

Most possessives change ending by gender and number, exactly like a regular adjective
(`adjective-forms`):

| | en-word | ett-word | plural |
|---|---|---|---|
| my | min bil | mitt hus | mina böcker |
| your (sg.) | din bil | ditt hus | dina böcker |
| our | vår bil | vårt hus | våra böcker |

`hans` (his), `hennes` (her), `dess` (its), `deras` (their) do **not** inflect — they stay the
same regardless of what follows. See `min-mitt` for a focused drill on the min/mitt/mina
pattern.

## sin vs. hans/hennes — the classic error

Swedish has a **reflexive possessive**, `sin/sitt/sina`, used only when the possessor is the
subject of the same clause. Russian's own reflexive `свой` makes this instinctive for a Russian
speaker but it is one of the hardest points for an English speaker, whose "his/her" does not
distinguish the two cases at all.

```example
Anna älskar sin man. — Anna loves her (own) husband.
Anna älskar hennes man. — Anna loves her (someone else's) husband.
```

The rule: if the "her" refers back to the subject of the sentence, use `sin`; if it refers to a
different woman entirely, use `hennes`.

## Demonstrative, relative and impersonal

- **Demonstrative** — `den här/det här/de här` ("this/these"), `den där/det där/de där`
  ("that/those"). See `det-har-dar`.
- **Relative** — `som` covers both "who" and "that/which" in Swedish, with no separate word for
  people versus things: `mannen som bor här` (the man who lives here), `boken som jag läser`
  (the book that I'm reading). See `relative-clauses`.
- **Impersonal** — `man` corresponds to English's generic "you" or "one": `Man får inte röka
  här` ("You're not allowed to smoke here" — nobody in particular). See `impersonal-det` for
  `man` alongside the other impersonal construction, formal `det`.
