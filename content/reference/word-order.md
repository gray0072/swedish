# Word order and the V2 rule

The single most important structural feature of a Swedish sentence is the **V2 rule**
(verb-second): in a statement, the finite verb almost always sits in **second position**,
no matter what occupies the first.

If the sentence starts with the subject, the order looks ordinary, the way it does in English:

```example
Jag bor i Stockholm. — I live in Stockholm.
```

But if the sentence starts with anything else — a time expression, a place, a whole
subordinate clause — the subject and verb **swap places**, so the verb stays second:

```example
Idag bor jag i Stockholm. — Today I live in Stockholm.
Imorgon reser vi till Göteborg. — Tomorrow we're travelling to Gothenburg.
```

Notice that `idag` comes first, not `jag` — so the verb `bor` stays in second position and the
subject `jag` is pushed to third. English allows the same sentence with the subject still
first (*"Today I live in Stockholm"*); Swedish does not have that freedom. This is not a
stylistic choice, it is a grammatical rule, and it is why V2 causes more beginner mistakes than
almost anything else in the language.

## A satsschema — the sentence laid out as a grid

| Position 1 | Verb | Subject (if not in pos. 1) | inte / adverb | Rest |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jag | bor | | inte | i Göteborg. |
| Idag | bor | jag | inte | i Göteborg. |
| Imorgon | reser | vi | | till Göteborg. |

Whatever sits in position 1 — subject, time word, or an entire subordinate clause — the verb
is locked into position 2. This grid is the tool to reach for whenever a sentence "sounds
wrong": find the verb, count to two.

## Questions

In yes/no questions, the verb moves to first position:

```example
Bor du i Stockholm? — Do you live in Stockholm?
```

In questions with a question word (who, what, where…), the verb stays in second position,
right after the question word:

```example
Var bor du? — Where do you live?
```

## Negation

**Inte** ("not") in a simple main clause comes **after** the finite verb, not before it the
way Russian and English both put their negation:

```example
Jag förstår inte. — I don't understand.
```

## Subordinate clauses: the BIFF rule

Inside a subordinate clause (after `att`, `om`, `när`, `eftersom`…), `inte` and other
sentence adverbs move **before** the verb instead of after it — the opposite of a main clause.
**BIFF** is the memory aid: *Bisats Inte Följer Finita verbet* — "in a subordinate clause,
*inte* comes before the finite verb."

```example
Jag vet att han inte kommer. — I know that he isn't coming.
```

This flip is the reason a sentence can sound perfect in isolation and wrong the moment it is
embedded in another one. See `conjunctions-and-connectors` for the full list of words that
open a subordinate clause and trigger BIFF.
