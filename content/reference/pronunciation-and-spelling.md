# Pronunciation and spelling

Swedish spelling is largely phonetic, but three features have no real counterpart in Russian
or English and each one causes real misunderstandings, not just an accent.

## Vowel length changes meaning

Every Swedish vowel has a long and a short version, and the difference is not decorative — it
changes the word. A stressed vowel is long when it is followed by at most one consonant, and
short when followed by two or more.

```example
vila (long i) — to rest
villa (short i) — house/villa
```

```example
väg (long ä) — road
vägg (short ä) — wall
```

Getting this wrong does not make you sound foreign so much as it makes you say a different
word — `en sil` (a strainer) versus `en sill` (a herring) is a genuine source of confusion at a
Swedish dinner table.

## The sj-sound and the tj-sound

Two consonant sounds have no equivalent letter in Russian or English and are spelled several
different ways each, which is why they are learned as sounds first and spellings second.

| Sound | Spelled as | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **sj-sound** (a soft, breathy "sh"/"kh") | sj, skj, stj, sk (before e/i/y/ä/ö), ch | sju, skjorta, stjärna, skida, chef |
| **tj-sound** (like "ch" without the t-stop) | tj, kj, k (before e/i/y/ä/ö) | tjej, kjol, köpa, kök |

Both sounds are consistently mis-spelled by beginners going the other direction — writing
`sju` as `hju`, for instance — because English offers no letter combination that maps cleanly
onto either sound.

## Sentence melody

Swedish has **pitch accent**: many two-syllable words are distinguished only by which syllable
carries a rising or falling tone (`anden` — the duck — versus `anden` — the spirit — are spelled
identically). This is close to impossible to master from text; it is best absorbed by listening
to whole sentences, which is exactly what the everyday dialogues in
[DIALOGUES.md](../../DIALOGUES.md) are for.

## Letters that lie

A few letters do not say what a Russian or English reader expects on sight: **g** before
e/i/y/ä/ö sounds like "y" (`ge`, `gilla`); **k** before the same vowels sounds like the
tj-sound above (`köpa`); **o** is frequently pronounced like a Russian "u" (`bok` sounds close
to "book" in vowel quality, not like an English "o"); and a doubled consonant is not decorative
— it is the marker that the preceding vowel is short (see above).

## In connected speech

Spoken Swedish drops and merges sounds that written Swedish keeps whole: `det är` collapses to
something close to *de'ä*, `sedan` is almost always said as `sen`, and `någonting` as
`nånting`. `connected-speech-reductions` and the everyday dialogues are where these actually
get heard, not just described.
