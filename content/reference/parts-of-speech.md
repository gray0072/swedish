# Parts of speech

SVA grundläggande teaches *in* this vocabulary — a Swedish class talks about a text using
these words — so it is worth knowing the terms even before every rule behind them is learned.

| Swedish | English | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **substantiv** | noun | bok, Stockholm, kärlek |
| **verb** | verb | springa, är, har blivit |
| **adjektiv** | adjective | stor, röd, snabbare |
| **adverb** | adverb | snabbt, ofta, tyvärr |
| **pronomen** | pronoun | jag, den, sig, som |
| **preposition** | preposition | i, på, till, med |
| **konjunktion** | conjunction (coordinating) | och, men, för, så |
| **subjunktion** | subordinating conjunction | att, om, när, eftersom |
| **räkneord** | numeral | tre, tredje, många |
| **interjektion** | interjection | oj, hej, aj |

## Why the distinction between konjunktion and subjunktion matters

Swedish grammar treats coordinating and subordinating conjunctions as two separate word
classes, not one — and this distinction is not cosmetic: a `subjunktion` opens a subordinate
clause, and a subordinate clause reorders the sentence (§ `word-order`, the BIFF rule), while a
`konjunktion` simply joins two main clauses with no such effect.

```example
Jag är trött, men jag jobbar vidare. — konjunktion: two main clauses, ordinary order.
Jag jobbar vidare fast jag är trött. — subjunktion: a subordinate clause follows "fast".
```

## Nouns and verbs carry the most grammar

Of the ten classes above, two do almost all of the structural work a learner has to master:
the noun (gender, definite form, plural — `noun-forms`) and the verb (four groups, six-odd
forms, the passive — `verb-forms`). Adjectives agree with both (`adjective-forms`); everything
else is largely invariant, which is one more way Swedish is lighter than Russian.
