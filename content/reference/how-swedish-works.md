# How Swedish works

Swedish is a North Germanic language, closer to English and German than to Russian in its
architecture, and it drops several things that make other languages hard.

## What Swedish does not have

- **No noun cases.** Russian marks a noun's role with six cases; Swedish marks it with word
  order and prepositions instead, the way English does. `Jag ser Anna` / `Anna ser mig` — the
  noun itself never changes, only the pronoun does (`jag` → `mig`).
- **No verb conjugation by person.** One verb form covers *I, you, he, she, we, they*:
  `jag talar`, `du talar`, `de talar` — all the same. Compare Russian's six endings for one
  verb, or even English's lone `-s` on *he speaks*. This is the single biggest shortcut
  Swedish grammar gives a beginner.
- **Only two genders**, not three: **en**-words and **ett**-words (§ `noun-genders`), not
  masculine/feminine/neuter as in Russian.

## What Swedish does that neither Russian nor English does

- **The definite article is a suffix, not a separate word.** "The book" is not two words but
  one: `bok` → `boken`. There is no Swedish word that means bare "the" the way English has one.
  See `noun-forms` for the full pattern.
- **Verb-second word order (V2) is a hard rule, not a stylistic option.** Whatever occupies
  first position, the finite verb must be second — which means the subject and verb swap places
  whenever anything other than the subject starts the sentence. See `word-order` for the
  mechanics; this is the rule that causes the most beginner mistakes, because Russian and
  English both allow this to be optional or absent.

```example
Jag bor i Stockholm. — I live in Stockholm.
Idag bor jag i Stockholm. — Today I live in Stockholm.
```

- **Vowel length changes meaning**, not just accent: `vila` (rest) and `villa` (house) differ
  only in how long the first vowel is held. See `pronunciation-and-spelling`.

## The real wall

The grammar above is genuinely easier than Russian's case system. What actually slows learners
down is **vocabulary that looks deceptively close to English** (`en sopa` is not "soup",
`glass` is ice cream) and the **listening gap**: spoken Swedish drops sounds, merges words and
uses a singing intonation that written Swedish hides completely — see
`pronunciation-and-spelling` and the everyday dialogues in [DIALOGUES.md](../../DIALOGUES.md)
for what it actually sounds like.
